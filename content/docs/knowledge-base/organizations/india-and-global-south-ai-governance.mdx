## Overview

**India and Global South AI Governance** refers to the coordinated effort by India and a coalition of developing nations — spanning Africa, Latin America, South Asia, and Southeast Asia — to reshape global AI regulation around development priorities rather than the safety-first or liability-focused frameworks championed by the United States and the European Union. The central claim of this movement is that countries representing the majority of the world's population have been systematically excluded from the rule-making processes that will govern the most consequential technology of the coming decades, and that this exclusion is neither ethically defensible nor practically stable.[^1]

India has positioned itself as the primary convenor and strategic bridge for this agenda. Its approach, articulated through NITI Aayog's foundational **#AIforAll** strategy, the **IndiaAI Mission**, and the November 2025 **India AI Governance Guidelines**, emphasizes incentivizing innovation and building domestic AI infrastructure rather than imposing prescriptive regulation comparable to the EU AI Act. India's governance philosophy rests on seven principles — Trust, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness and Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, and Safety, Resilience and Sustainability — and explicitly frames economic inclusion and sovereignty as inseparable from responsible AI development.[^2]

The broader Global South governance agenda extends well beyond India. Brazil's Bill 2338/2023 and its 2025 BRICS presidency declaration on AI, the African Union's AI Strategy 2024 and nascent African AI Council concept, ASEAN's Guide on AI Governance and Ethics 2024, Kenya's AI Strategy 2024, and emerging positions from Indonesia, Nigeria, and Vietnam collectively represent a significant reorientation of <EntityLink id="E154">AI governance and policy</EntityLink> away from a largely transatlantic conversation. These actors matter not only because of demographic weight — the Global South contains the majority of the world's AI users and workers — but because of their outsized role in global data generation, the strategic dynamics of the US-China technology competition, and the expanding network of national AI Safety Institutes that requires their participation to be globally legitimate.[^3]

## History and Background

### NITI Aayog and the #AIforAll Foundation

India's formal engagement with AI governance began with NITI Aayog's **National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence**, which established the foundational concept of **#AIforAll** — framing India's AI leadership as oriented toward inclusive development rather than narrow commercial or military advantage. This strategy positioned AI across priority sectors including agriculture, healthcare, education, and smart mobility, emphasizing India's unique potential to demonstrate that AI adoption at scale could serve social equity goals.[^2]

The strategy also reflected a pragmatic recognition of India's structural advantages: a massive and linguistically diverse population generating large volumes of data, a substantial software engineering talent pool, and existing Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) in the form of Aadhaar (biometric identity), UPI (payments), and DigiLocker (document storage). These systems, collectively called India Stack, provided a governance-adjacent infrastructure that no other developing country had assembled at comparable scale.[^4]

### IndiaAI Mission and Institutional Development

The **IndiaAI Mission**, approved by the Union Cabinet in March 2024, operationalized this strategy through concrete infrastructure investment. The Mission funds a public compute pool of approximately 18,000 GPUs, establishing a precedent for state-enabled democratization of AI infrastructure that has attracted attention from African and Latin American governments as a potential model for shared regional compute.[^5] The Mission also supports **BharatGen**, a multilingual generative AI initiative targeting India's 22 official languages, and the **Bhashini** platform for natural language processing across Indian languages — both addressing the systematic underrepresentation of non-English languages in global AI training data.

In March 2024, MeitY (the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology) issued an initial advisory requiring platforms to obtain explicit permission before deploying under-tested AI models and to include disclaimers about potential unreliability. Following industry pushback, this advisory was withdrawn and replaced with a less restrictive version — an early indicator of the tension between India's pro-innovation orientation and the precautionary impulses that periodically emerge within Indian regulatory circles.[^2]

### The India AI Governance Guidelines (November 2025)

In November 2025, MeitY released the **India AI Governance Guidelines**, which became India's primary framework for responsible AI adoption. The document superseded prior policy efforts and introduced a "hands-off, self-regulatory" approach, favoring voluntary measures such as self-certifications and regulatory sandboxes over binding legislative mandates. The Guidelines also established three pillars for governance action: infrastructure development, capacity building, and accountability mechanisms — a structure explicitly designed to be transferable to other Global South contexts.[^2]

The Indian government has also drafted but not yet enacted the **Digital India Act**, which would update the country's foundational IT law to address AI-specific concerns. India currently lacks a codified AI-specific legislation, relying instead on the IT Act, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, and the November 2025 Guidelines as its primary governance instruments.[^6]

### India AI Impact Summit 2026

In February 2026, India hosted the **India AI Impact Summit** in New Delhi — the fourth in a global series following Bletchley Park (2023), Seoul (2024), and Paris (2025), and notably the first hosted in the Global South. The Summit ran from February 16-20, with Prime Minister Narendra Modi formally inaugurating proceedings on February 19. Its final declaration garnered 89 signatories, exceeding the 60 endorsements from the 2025 Paris AI Summit.[^7]

The Summit showcased three sovereign AI models, secured major Big Tech infrastructure pledges, and formalized South-South working groups on compute access, multilingual data, AI literacy, and governance experimentation. It also marked India's formal launch of an **AI Safety Institute** (IndiaAI AISI), joining the growing international network of national AI Safety Institutes and signaling that India intends to participate in technical AI safety discussions, not only governance and development ones.[^7]

## Key Activities and Initiatives

### National Governance Frameworks

India's governance model has attracted analytical attention as a potential template for the Global South, specifically because it avoids the resource-intensive compliance infrastructure that the EU AI Act requires. A study examining AI ethics policies from ten Global South countries — Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Egypt, India, Indonesia, Mauritius, Malaysia, Thailand, and Qatar — identified **equity** and **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities** as common ethical principles across these frameworks, and argued that an India-led development-centric model served these countries' collective interests better than adoption of EU or US frameworks.[^8]

Research examining this cluster of governance approaches identifies a structural pattern: out of approximately 500 AI policies published between 2011 and 2023, only about 7% originated from Latin America or Africa. The Global South has historically functioned as a rule-taker rather than rule-maker, absorbing governance frameworks designed for contexts with very different infrastructure, labor markets, and institutional capacities.[^9]

### Digital Public Infrastructure as a Governance Model

India's integration of AI with its Digital Public Infrastructure represents a distinctive governance contribution distinct from pure regulatory frameworks. Aadhaar's biometric identity system, UPI's payment network (now incorporating AI-powered voice transactions), and open health data systems like the Open Healthcare Network collectively constitute a public AI deployment architecture with implications for accountability, auditability, and equitable access that pure regulatory approaches cannot achieve.[^4]

India has actively exported this DPI model diplomatically — sharing India Stack architecture with Nepal, Bhutan, Singapore, and several African countries — positioning AI governance not as a set of rules but as an embedded property of public digital systems. According to research on this approach, the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) system exemplifies how AI-integrated DPI can reduce corruption through traceable digital payment trails, with potential scalability to lower-income governance contexts.[^4]

### South-South Cooperation Frameworks

The Africa–Asia AI Policymaker Network, established in 2022, provides a platform for peer learning on AI policy across the two continents, with particular focus on regulatory experimentation in contexts underserved by Global North frameworks. The 2026 Summit formalized issue-specific South-South working groups proposing to link India's IndiaAI infrastructure with Brazil's public computing institutions and African regional hosting initiatives.[^5]

The **Africa Just AI Project** (run by Research ICT Africa) represents the research dimension of this cooperation, emphasizing participatory processes grounded in citizens' lived realities and prioritizing immediate harms prevalent in the Global South — including AI-enabled targeted hate, discrimination, and erosion of democratic fundamentals — rather than the frontier risk scenarios that dominate Global North safety discourse.[^10] The project also advocates for "virtuous data production" through local language broadcaster archives and community data exchanges, creating sovereign datasets for culturally nuanced local large language models.

### Brazil, ASEAN, Africa, and Emerging Actors

**Brazil** has been the most legislatively active large developing economy on AI governance. Bill 2338/2023 represents a comprehensive AI regulatory proposal under development, and Brazil's 2025 BRICS presidency produced a declaration explicitly warning that AI risks deepening global inequalities without collective UN-coordinated action. Brazil's National Data Protection Authority (ANPD) and the Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation (MCTI) are the primary institutional actors.[^11]

**ASEAN** published its **Guide on AI Governance and Ethics** in 2024 as part of its broader Digital Master Plan, providing a regional soft-law framework for member states at very different levels of AI development. The guide emphasizes proportionate regulation and Southeast Asian contextual needs, including multilingual AI and agricultural applications.[^3]

The **African Union's AI Strategy 2024** and the emerging concept of an **African AI Council** represent the continental governance layer, aiming to give African nations collective voice in global forums while addressing the severe compute and data infrastructure deficits that make any purely regulatory approach insufficient. Kenya's AI Strategy 2024 and the Konza Technopolis initiative represent national-level implementation, while South Africa's AI Institute and its leadership of the G20 AI agenda in 2025 gave the continent an additional seat at high-table discussions.[^3]

**Indonesia**, **Nigeria**, and **Vietnam** are all developing AI policy positions, though their frameworks remain less institutionally advanced than India's or Brazil's. Their significance is demographic and strategic: collectively they represent hundreds of millions of AI users, major data generation nodes, and important actors in any attempt to build globally legitimate AI governance.[^3]

### The 'AI for Development' Agenda vs. AI Safety

A critical conceptual distinction runs through Global South AI governance: the **AI for Development** framing treats AI primarily as a tool for economic growth, poverty reduction, agricultural productivity, and public service delivery — applications where risks of over-restriction are considered more serious than risks of under-regulation. This contrasts sharply with the **AI Safety** framing that has dominated discourse in the United States and United Kingdom, which prioritizes frontier model risks, existential hazards, and alignment failures as the central governance challenges.[^12]

The <EntityLink id="E154">AI governance</EntityLink> implications of this divide are significant. Global South actors have generally supported the expansion of the international AI Safety Institute network, including India's own AISI, while simultaneously arguing that the safety agenda has been narrowly defined around concerns most relevant to frontier AI developers concentrated in a handful of wealthy countries. Research from the MAP-AI initiative and the Global Digital Justice Forum advocates for "lifecycle accountability" approaches that address immediate, concrete harms — discrimination, surveillance misuse, disinformation — as the appropriate safety priority for Global South contexts.[^10]

## Key People

- **Narendra Modi (Prime Minister of India)**: Frames India's AI agenda primarily around economic growth and Big Tech partnership. Co-chaired the 2025 Paris AI Action Summit with French President Macron; inaugurated the 2026 India AI Impact Summit. His government's approach has been characterized by critics as prioritizing industrial policy over equitable governance.[^7]
- **Nandan Nilekani**: Architect of Aadhaar and India Stack; influential in shaping the DPI-as-governance model that underlies India's distinctive approach to AI deployment accountability. His influence on MeitY and IndiaAI Mission design is widely noted in policy circles, though the specific contours of his current advisory role are not fully documented in public sources.[^4]
- **Global South Alliance (GSA)**: Civil society coalition that submitted a pre-Summit letter advocating for institutionalized multistakeholder participation mechanisms. The letter's recommendations were not incorporated into the Summit design, a point of sustained criticism.[^10]
- **Research ICT Africa (Africa Just AI Project)**: Brought participatory governance evidence from the Africa Just AI research program to the Summit, representing civil society perspectives grounded in African deployment contexts.[^10]

## Criticism

Assessments of India's Global South AI governance leadership — including from sympathetic researchers — identify several significant shortcomings that complicate the more optimistic framing of India as a development-centric alternative to Western governance models.

**Missed institutionalization at the Summit**: Despite raising global expectations as the first AI summit hosted in the Global South, the 2026 India AI Impact Summit produced a Leaders' Declaration with no binding commitments and limited clarity on implementation pathways. Critics note that the event reproduced patterns from prior summits — government-Big Tech showcasing — without advancing the structural governance needs most relevant to developing countries.[^10] The Summit also attracted diplomatic embarrassment, including Bill Gates withdrawing from a scheduled keynote, though the specific reasons for this withdrawal are not publicly documented.[^13]

**Corporate-government dominance over civil society**: Despite rhetoric about multistakeholderism, no structured mechanisms ensured meaningful participation by Global South civil society or academic institutions in the negotiated outcomes. The Summit's primary deliverables centered on Big Tech investment pledges aligned with Modi's economic growth agenda, and the Global South Alliance's calls for institutionalized participation were ignored.[^10]

**Regulatory gaps and enforcement deficits**: India currently lacks codified AI legislation, binding regulatory frameworks, a dedicated AI regulatory body, or a legal definition of AI. Broader Global South governance suffers from weak data protection regimes, compute and data concentration in external cloud providers, and institutional readiness deficits that make voluntary governance guidelines insufficient.[^6] The March 2024 advisory withdrawal following industry pressure illustrates the political difficulty of maintaining even soft regulatory positions against commercial interests.

**The 'magical cure' problem**: Research examining AI governance across Global South countries identifies a pattern of treating AI as a solution to development challenges without building the localized governance, digital literacy, or R&D infrastructure necessary to deploy it responsibly. This risks positioning developing countries as passive consumers and testing grounds for technologies designed elsewhere.[^9]

**Terminology and homogenization**: Multiple researchers note that "Global South" in AI discourse risks implying false homogeneity across vastly different countries — conflating India's 1.4 billion people and \$3.5 trillion economy with much smaller, lower-capacity states — in ways that obscure the actual diversity of governance needs and capabilities.[^8]

**The Trump administration's opposition**: At an India AI summit, the Trump administration's representatives explicitly rejected global AI governance frameworks, highlighting the structural difficulty of any India-led effort to build binding multilateral consensus when the primary AI superpower opts out.[^13]

**Workforce risks**: Unchecked AI adoption threatens India's approximately 900,000 annual engineering graduates and the country's large informal labor sector, with analysts warning that industrial policy focused on Big Tech infrastructure investment may accelerate automation without adequate transition support.[^6]

## Key Uncertainties

- Whether the South-South working groups formalized at the 2026 Summit will achieve concrete coordination outcomes, or dissipate as previous summit-adjacent initiatives have
- Whether India's AISI will meaningfully bridge the "AI for Development" and "AI Safety" agendas, or remain focused on governance rather than technical safety research
- Whether Brazil's Bill 2338/2023 will be enacted and, if so, whether it will provide a legislative template for other Latin American and African countries
- Whether the AU AI Strategy 2024 and African AI Council concept will achieve sufficient institutional backing to function as a real counterweight to Global North standard-setting bodies
- The extent to which India's DPI model is genuinely transferable to lower-capacity states given the unique political, institutional, and demographic conditions that enabled its construction
