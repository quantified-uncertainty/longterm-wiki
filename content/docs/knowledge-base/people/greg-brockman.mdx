## Overview

<EntityLink id="E2044">Greg Brockman</EntityLink> is an American software engineer, entrepreneur, and co-founder of <EntityLink id="E218">OpenAI</EntityLink>, where he currently serves as President and leads the company's Scaling organization. He is one of the central figures in the development of large-scale AI systems, having been involved in the creation and deployment of GPT-4, ChatGPT, DALL-E, and Codex. Colleagues have described him as one of the hardest-working engineers at OpenAI, reportedly spending the majority of his time coding directly.[^1]

Brockman grew up in Thompson, North Dakota, where he excelled academically — placing 6th in the 2007 Intel Science Talent Search and earning a silver medal in the 2006 International Chemistry Olympiad.[^2] After brief stints at Harvard and MIT, he dropped out in 2010 to join Stripe as an early engineer, eventually becoming its first CTO in 2013. He left Stripe in 2015 to co-found OpenAI alongside Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Ilya Sutskever, and others, motivated in part by concern over the concentration of AI research within large corporations such as Google, which had acquired <EntityLink id="E98">Google DeepMind</EntityLink> in 2014.[^3]

Within the AI safety community, Brockman occupies a complicated position. OpenAI was founded explicitly as a nonprofit oriented toward safe AGI development, and Brockman has consistently articulated commitments to iterative deployment, safety testing, and broad distribution of AI's benefits. However, the organization has also attracted sustained criticism — including from former employees — over whether commercial pressures have displaced safety priorities. A private diary entry from 2017, later surfaced in legal proceedings, raised direct questions about the sincerity of OpenAI's original nonprofit commitments.[^4]

## Early Life and Education

Brockman was born on November 29, 1987, in Thompson, North Dakota, the son of Ellen Feldman and Ronald Brockman.[^2] He attended Red River High School, where he was captain of a state-champion Science Bowl team and was involved in drumline, math club, Latin club, and contributed to a local paper's Teen Page.[^2] His academic achievements during this period were exceptional: he placed 6th in the 2007 Intel Science Talent Search for a paper titled "Asymptotic Behavior of Certain Ducci Sequences," published in *Fibonacci Quarterly*; won a silver medal at the 2006 International Chemistry Olympiad (reportedly the first Intel STS finalist from North Dakota since 1973); and was a semi-finalist in the Physics Olympiad.[^2] He also attended Canada/USA Mathcamp in 2003, 2005, and 2007.[^2]

According to his own account, Brockman became seriously interested in programming during a gap year after high school, inspired by Alan Turing's essay "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." He built a chatbot during this period but found himself confronted with the limitations of AI at the time.[^5]

He enrolled at Harvard University in 2008, intending to double-major in mathematics and computer science, and was active in the Harvard Computer Society. He later transferred to MIT, where he worked on projects including XVM, Linerva, and scripts.mit.edu, before dropping out in 2010 to join Stripe.[^2]

## Career

### Stripe (2010–2015)

Brockman joined Stripe in 2010 as its fourth employee — the company was co-founded by Patrick Collison, an MIT classmate — and spent five years helping scale the company from a handful of employees to over 200.[^6] He became Stripe's first CTO in 2013. He departed in May 2015, citing a desire to work on problems he considered more consequential than developer payment infrastructure.[^6]

### Co-founding OpenAI (2015)

Brockman was introduced to Sam Altman through Patrick Collison in 2011, and the two later connected with Elon Musk around their shared concern that AI development was becoming concentrated in corporate hands — particularly following Google's \$500 million acquisition of DeepMind in 2014.[^3] Brockman has described committing immediately to their vision of a nonprofit AI lab oriented toward safe AGI development for all of humanity.

OpenAI was formally co-founded in December 2015 with eleven founding members: Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, Trevor Blackwell, Vicki Cheung, Andrej Karpathy, Durk Kingma, John Schulman, Pamela Vagata, and Wojciech Zaremba.[^3] Early operations were run from Brockman's living room, with \$1 billion in committed funding from Musk, Altman, Peter Thiel, and others.[^3] Brockman served as the organization's first CTO and, while Altman was still formally at Y Combinator, functioned in some respects as the effective operational lead.[^3]

Brockman's role at OpenAI has been primarily infrastructural and engineering-focused. He led recruitment efforts in the early years, including securing Ilya Sutskever from Google Brain. He oversaw projects including OpenAI Gym (2016), OpenAI Five (the Dota 2 AI), and the GPT series. On March 14, 2023, he conducted the live demonstration of GPT-4.[^7]

### 2023 Board Crisis

On November 17, 2023, OpenAI's board fired CEO Sam Altman, citing concerns about transparency, and simultaneously removed Brockman from his position as board chairman. Brockman resigned as president in response. Within days, roughly 95% of OpenAI employees signed a petition demanding reinstatement of Altman and Brockman, and both returned — Altman as CEO, Brockman as President — by November 21, 2023.[^8] Brockman briefly announced he would join Microsoft alongside Altman during the intervening period.[^8]

Accounts from the period describe internal allegations including claims from then-CTO Mira Murati that Brockman's close relationship with Altman had interfered with her ability to do her job, and a document from Ilya Sutskever containing bullying-related allegations.[^8] Brockman has not publicly addressed these specific claims in detail.

### Sabbatical and Return (2024)

Brockman announced a sabbatical beginning August 5, 2024, describing it as his first extended break since co-founding OpenAI nearly a decade earlier.[^9] He returned on November 12, 2024, taking on a formalized role leading what OpenAI calls the "Scaling" group — focused on the infrastructure underpinning AI development, including chips, data centers, software systems, and operations.[^9]

### Current Role

As President and head of the Scaling organization, Brockman oversees what OpenAI has described as a \$1.4 trillion commitment to build out approximately 30 gigawatts of compute capacity, encompassing data centers in Texas, New Mexico, Norway, and Argentina.[^10] In October 2025, he announced a multiyear partnership with AMD, involving hundreds of thousands of chips, aimed at diversifying OpenAI's compute supply away from dependence on NVIDIA.[^10]

## Views on AI and AGI

Brockman has expressed strong conviction that AGI is achievable and will be transformative. In various public appearances, he has stated that there is "no bend in the scaling laws" — meaning that AI performance continues to improve predictably with larger models, more compute, and more data — and that execution rather than fundamental obstacles is the primary challenge.[^11] He has compared the scale of infrastructure required for frontier AI to dwarfing the Apollo program and has predicted that GDP growth in countries will increasingly be driven by available compute.[^11]

On AI safety specifically, Brockman has emphasized iterative deployment and safety testing at the model level. He has noted that OpenAI spent over six months on alignment and safety testing for GPT-4 prior to deployment, including using the model itself in safety pipelines.[^12] He has advocated for international governance frameworks for superintelligence, drawing an analogy to the IAEA, and has argued that the risks of multiple independent actors developing AI make a coordinated global approach preferable to attempting to halt development entirely.[^12]

Brockman has also connected OpenAI's for-profit transition to its safety mission, arguing that access to capital is a prerequisite for the computational infrastructure needed to develop safe AGI, and that capitalism can be a useful mechanism for deploying powerful technology within appropriate bounds.[^3]

## Publications

Brockman's academic and technical output spans two periods. Prior to OpenAI, his work was primarily in mathematics:

- "On Universal Cycles of Labeled Graphs" (2010, *Electronic Journal of Combinatorics*, with Bill Kay and Emma E. Snively)[^13]
- "Asymptotic Behavior of Certain Ducci Sequences" (2007/2009, with R. Zerr)[^13]
- "Omnibus Sequences, Coupon Collection, and Missing Word Counts" (2011, with Sunil Abraham, S. Sapp, and A. Godbole)[^13]

At OpenAI, he has been a co-author on a range of AI/ML publications, including:

- "OpenAI Gym" (2016)[^13]
- "Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code" (2021)[^13]
- "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision" (ICML 2023, with Alec Radford, Jong Wook Kim, and others)[^13]
- "OpenAI o1 System Card" (2024)[^13]
- "Systems and Algorithms for Convolutional Multi-Hybrid Language Models at Scale" (2025, with multiple OpenAI affiliates)[^13]

## Criticism and Controversies

### The Elon Musk Lawsuit

The most significant legal controversy involving Brockman stems from a federal lawsuit filed by Elon Musk, alleging that Brockman and Sam Altman manipulated Musk into co-founding OpenAI as a nonprofit by promising transparency and safety-first governance, then secretly planned a transition to a for-profit structure.[^4]

Central to the lawsuit is a private diary entry Brockman wrote in 2017, surfaced through legal discovery. In it, he wrote that OpenAI's nonprofit commitment was "a lie," noted that it would be "morally bankrupt" to convert without Musk's knowledge, and described internal discussions about wanting Musk out of the organization.[^4] He also noted, under a section labeled "our plan," that the team had been considering whether to "flip to a for profit."[^4] OpenAI acknowledged the authenticity of the journal but did not directly rebut its contents.[^4]

A federal judge denied dismissal motions in January 2026, clearing the case for jury trial in April 2026 in Oakland.[^4] The case involves Brockman, Altman, and Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella as defendants.

### Safety Culture Concerns

In May 2024, Jan Leike — who had co-led OpenAI's superalignment team — resigned, publicly stating that safety culture and processes had been deprioritized in favor of product development. Brockman and Altman responded with statements affirming OpenAI's ongoing safety research but offered limited specifics.[^12] The superalignment team was subsequently disbanded.[^12] AI researcher Geoffrey Hinton, among others, has raised concerns about AI risk that implicitly challenge the adequacy of OpenAI's current safety approach.[^12]

### Nonprofit-to-For-Profit Transition

Within the <EntityLink id="E538">LessWrong</EntityLink> and Effective Altruism communities, OpenAI's structural evolution has generated ongoing criticism. Some community members have argued that OpenAI recruited talent under an EA-aligned nonprofit mission and then restructured in ways that primarily benefited insiders. Calls have been made for organizations such as <EntityLink id="E510">80,000 Hours</EntityLink> to stop directing talent toward OpenAI unless senior leaders make credible safety commitments.[^14] Brockman's donation of \$25 million (with his wife) to a Trump-aligned Super PAC, and \$50 million to a separate AI deregulation Super PAC co-founded with Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz, have further complicated his standing with parts of the AI safety community.[^14]

### 2023 Internal Allegations

During the November 2023 board crisis, internal documents and statements alleged that Brockman had engaged in bullying behavior, and that his close relationship with Altman had created structural problems for other senior leaders.[^8] These allegations were not the stated basis for the board's actions, and Brockman returned to the company without a public resolution of the underlying concerns.

## Key Uncertainties

- The extent to which Brockman's 2017 diary entries reflect a deliberate scheme to defraud Musk, versus uncertainty about organizational structure during a chaotic founding period, remains contested and will likely be litigated at trial in 2026.
- The degree to which Brockman personally shapes OpenAI's safety practices — versus Altman or the broader research leadership — is not well-documented in public sources.
- Whether OpenAI's current infrastructure-focused strategy under Brockman's Scaling organization is consistent with or in tension with its stated AI safety mission is debated both internally (per Leike's departure) and externally.
- Brockman's political donations and stated support for AI deregulation policies represent a potential shift in his public positioning that has not been fully reconciled with his earlier framing of OpenAI as a safety-first nonprofit.
