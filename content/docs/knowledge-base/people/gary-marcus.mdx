## Overview

<EntityLink id="E148">Gary Marcus</EntityLink> is an American cognitive scientist, psychologist, author, and entrepreneur who has become one of the most prominent public skeptics of contemporary artificial intelligence. Trained at MIT under Steven Pinker, he spent much of his academic career as a professor of psychology and neural science at New York University, where he is now Professor Emeritus. His research spans human and animal cognition, language acquisition, neuroscience, and evolutionary psychology, and he has published extensively in journals including *Science* and *Nature*.[^1]

Marcus is perhaps best known publicly for his sustained critique of large language models (LLMs) and deep learning more broadly. He argues that current AI systems—despite impressive surface-level performance—lack the robust world models, reliable reasoning, and factual grounding needed for genuine intelligence or safe real-world deployment. He advocates instead for hybrid approaches that combine neural networks with symbolic AI methods, a position sometimes called neurosymbolic AI. These views have made him a frequent contributor to *The New Yorker*, *Wired*, and *The New York Times*, as well as the author of the Substack newsletter "Marcus on AI."[^2]

Beyond his academic and writing work, Marcus is a serial entrepreneur. He founded Geometric Intelligence, a machine learning startup acquired by Uber in December 2016, and later co-founded Robust.AI with robotics pioneer Rodney Brooks. He has also engaged directly with policymakers, testifying before the U.S. Senate on AI risks and signing the March 2023 open letter calling for a temporary moratorium on training AI systems more powerful than GPT-4.[^3]

## Background and Education

Marcus grew up in Baltimore, Maryland, and developed an early interest in artificial intelligence and programming. According to an interview, at age 10 he learned to program via a paper-based computer simulation in an after-school program for gifted students, an experience he later described to media as formative.[^4] He graduated high school early, reportedly after building a Latin-to-English translator.

He majored in cognitive science at Hampshire College before earning his PhD from MIT, where he was mentored by Steven Pinker. His doctoral research focused on negative evidence in language acquisition and the over-regularization of grammatical morphology in children—work that would inform his later skepticism about whether statistical pattern-matching alone can explain human language and cognition.[^1]

## Academic Career

At New York University, Marcus built a research program at the intersection of cognitive psychology, neuroscience, and AI. He is described in sources as perhaps the youngest person to hold the title of Professor Emeritus at NYU.[^5] His academic publications touch on human and animal behavior, genetics, linguistics, evolutionary psychology, and artificial intelligence. Early peer-reviewed work included a 1998 paper on out-of-distribution failures in multilayer perceptrons and a 1999 paper in *Science* arguing that infants extrapolate in ways that neural networks of the era could not replicate—research he has since cited as anticipating the limitations of modern deep learning.[^6]

Later academic contributions include a 2020 arXiv paper, "The Next Decade in AI: Four Steps Towards Robust Artificial Intelligence," which proposed a hybrid, knowledge-driven, reasoning-based approach drawing on cognitive science; a 2021 paper co-authored with Ernest Davis in *Communications of the ACM* titled "Insights for AI from the Human Mind"; and several 2022–2023 papers examining whether LLMs exhibit genuine sensitivity to linguistic meaning and physical reasoning.[^6]

## Entrepreneurial Ventures

In 2014, Marcus founded **Geometric Intelligence**, a machine learning startup of which he served as founder and CEO. In December 2016, Uber acquired the company, and Marcus became director of Uber's AI efforts—a role he held until March 2017.[^1]

In 2019, Marcus co-founded **Robust.AI** with Rodney Brooks, the iRobot co-founder and Roomba co-inventor. The company aims to develop an "off-the-shelf" machine-learning platform for autonomous robots, modeled on how video-game engines are adopted by third-party developers. Marcus has served as founder, CEO, and later Executive Chairman.[^3]

## Views on AI

### Critique of Large Language Models

Marcus has consistently argued that LLMs are unreliable, prone to hallucination, and lack genuine world models. He has described current generative AI as technically and morally inadequate, pointing to failures such as AI systems generating illegal chess moves, providing dangerous health misinformation, or failing basic logical reasoning tasks even when given algorithms to follow.[^7] He has referred to the epistemic effects of sycophantic AI as an "epistemic nightmare."

He draws a distinction between fluency—at which LLMs excel—and genuine intelligence, which he argues requires robust reasoning, reliable factual grounding, and the kind of compositional understanding that current systems lack. In a recurring argument, Marcus notes that if AI truly performed as its proponents claim, interpretability research and safety auditing would be unnecessary; the fact that real-world failures occur—including autonomous vehicle fatalities and harmful chatbot outputs—demonstrates the gap between marketing and reality.[^8]

### Advocacy for Neurosymbolic AI

Marcus argues that the field's overreliance on scaling data and model size has produced diminishing returns and diverts resources from more promising approaches. He advocates for neurosymbolic AI: systems that combine the pattern-recognition strengths of neural networks with the logical, rule-governed structure of symbolic AI. He contends this hybrid approach is more likely to yield robust, interpretable, and trustworthy AI systems, and has described his position as that of a "Generative AI realist" rather than a wholesale opponent of AI.[^2]

### Regulatory Positions

Marcus advocates for AI regulation analogous to FDA drug approval processes, including mandatory pre-deployment auditing, third-party testing, and liability frameworks. He has testified before the U.S. Senate on AI risks, proposed a CERN-like international AI safety initiative (originally in 2017), and signed the March 29, 2023 open letter calling for a six-month moratorium on training AI systems more powerful than GPT-4, citing concerns about unreliable AI being widely deployed before adequate safeguards exist.[^3][^9]

In 2024, he published *Taming Silicon Valley: How We Can Ensure That AI Works for Us*, a book urging public action to regulate generative AI.[^10]

### Economic and Investment Concerns

Marcus has warned that Silicon Valley's and the U.S. government's enthusiasm for generative AI—backed by massive investment in data centers and chips—may produce costly infrastructure built on speculative assumptions. He has compared the potential downside to previous financial crises and suggested that if the AI sector experiences a significant downturn, government bailouts could follow. He has argued that the economics of LLMs are fundamentally problematic, with research indicating that a large majority of companies report no significant return on AI investment.[^11]

### Position on Existential Risk

Marcus acknowledges risks from misaligned superhuman AI and bad actors exploiting AI advances, but is skeptical of rapid-takeoff scenarios and imminent superintelligence narratives. He has characterized scenarios like "AI 2027" as more fictional than scientific, and warned that such framings may backfire by promoting a sense of inevitability around superintelligence that ultimately benefits AI companies rather than advancing safety. His emphasis is on near-term regulatory preparedness rather than speculative long-term doomsday scenarios.[^9]

## Predictions and Track Record

Marcus has issued annual AI predictions since at least 2022, positioning them as tests of his analytical framework. He has claimed that 16 of 17 "high confidence" predictions for 2025 proved correct, including: no AGI achieved despite public claims by prominent figures; GPT-5 being underwhelming relative to hype; persistent LLM hallucinations; unreliable AI agents; and a peak in the AI investment bubble with Wall Street beginning to lose confidence. He has described 2025 as a year that validated his core 2022 thesis—articulated in an essay titled "Deep Learning Is Hitting a Wall"—that scaling alone would not resolve LLMs' fundamental limitations.[^12]

For 2026, Marcus predicted continued escalation in neurosymbolic AI and world-model research, no AGI, and a broader market backlash against generative AI hype. He noted a "metaprediction" that his 2026 forecasts would be less accurate than his 2025 ones, on the grounds that the field itself is becoming more realistic and his views more mainstream.[^13]

These self-reported track records should be interpreted with some caution. Marcus selects and frames his predictions, and some external observers have raised concerns about how success is measured.

## Publications

Marcus has authored or edited six books:

- *The Algebraic Mind* (2001)
- *The Birth of the Mind*
- *Kluge: The Haphazard Evolution of the Human Mind*
- *Guitar Zero* (New York Times bestseller)
- *Rebooting AI: Building Artificial Intelligence We Can Trust* (2019, co-authored with Ernest Davis; named one of Forbes's 7 Must-Read Books in AI)[^14]
- *Taming Silicon Valley: How We Can Ensure That AI Works for Us* (2024)[^10]

He has also edited *The Future of the Brain* and *The Norton Psychology Reader*, and is a frequent contributor to *The New Yorker*, *Wired*, and *The New York Times*. His Substack newsletter, "Marcus on AI," launched in May 2022, publishes ongoing critiques of AI development and industry trends.[^2]

## Criticisms and Controversies

### Goalpost-Moving Accusations

The most sustained criticism of Marcus from the AI research community is that he repeatedly shifts the goalposts of his critiques. Critics, including commentators on <EntityLink id="E538">LessWrong</EntityLink> and Hacker News, argue that each time a new model addresses the specific failures he highlighted, he moves on to identify new limitations without acknowledging that the prior concerns were resolved. For example, when GPT-3 addressed many failures Marcus had documented in GPT-2, and GPT-4 addressed many of those he had documented in GPT-3, critics argued this pattern suggests his framework cannot be falsified.[^15] Marcus's response is that the core issues—hallucinations, unreliable reasoning, lack of robust world models—remain unresolved across model generations, and that fluency improvements do not constitute the kind of progress he considers meaningful.

### Questions About Model Selection

Some critics have noted that Marcus has tested older or less capable models when demonstrating AI failures. A LessWrong post from 2024 highlighted that Marcus demonstrated reasoning failures using GPT-4o (the free tier) rather than GPT-o1, which reportedly solved the same tasks without errors.[^15] Marcus has maintained that failures on any widely deployed model are practically significant regardless of what premium-tier models can achieve.

### Vagueness of Alternatives

EA Forum discussions have characterized Marcus's critiques of deep learning as imprecise, noting that claims like "true understanding is needed" are difficult to evaluate scientifically without specification of what would constitute such understanding. Commentators in these discussions have observed that his arguments about what AI cannot do are frequently framed in ways that are hard to test or falsify.[^16]

### Good Faith Questions

Some commentators on Hacker News have raised questions about consistency in Marcus's positions over time, suggesting his stances align with whatever narrative attracts media attention. These are minority views in the available sources and are not substantiated with specific documented inconsistencies.[^17]

### Entrepreneurial Interests

Marcus has founded two machine learning companies—Geometric Intelligence and Robust.AI—which represent potential financial interests that could shape his public positions on AI development and regulation. He advocates for neurosymbolic AI and hybrid approaches that align with the technical direction of Robust.AI. He has acknowledged this tension indirectly, describing himself as "a big fan of AI" and stating that LLMs frustrate him partly because they distract from scientific work he considers more valuable.[^18]

## Community Reception

In EA Forum and LessWrong discussions, Marcus is generally treated as a credible voice on AI limitations but one whose critiques are seen as imprecise by technically-oriented readers. His AGI timeline estimates—roughly 10–20 years in some sources—are noted as not dramatically different from those of other prominent AI researchers, and his support for AI pauses and safety measures receives positive acknowledgment. His more specific technical arguments about deep learning's limitations are viewed by some community members as insufficiently specified to constitute clear falsifiable claims.[^16]

## Key Uncertainties

- Whether Marcus's preferred neurosymbolic approach can deliver the reliability and scalability he claims, or whether hybrid systems face their own fundamental limitations.
- How to properly evaluate his prediction track record, given that he controls the framing of what counts as a confirmed prediction.
- The degree to which his entrepreneurial interests in Robust.AI shape his public advocacy for neurosymbolic approaches over pure deep learning.
- Whether his regulatory proposals—FDA-like approval processes, international AI safety bodies—are practically achievable given the speed of AI development and the diffuse international nature of the field.
