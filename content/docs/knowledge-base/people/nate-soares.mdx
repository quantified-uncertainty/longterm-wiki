## Overview

Nate Soares (also known online as "so8res") is an AI safety researcher and the president of the <EntityLink id="E202">Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI)</EntityLink>, a Berkeley-based nonprofit focused on ensuring that advanced AI systems do not pose existential risks to humanity.[^1] He joined MIRI in April 2014 as a research fellow after leaving a software engineering position at Google, and quickly took on a central leadership role, eventually becoming executive director before transitioning to president in 2023.[^2]

Soares is widely regarded as one of the most prominent voices warning about catastrophic risks from artificial superintelligence. His technical work helped establish the conceptual foundations of what is now called "AI alignment"—the challenge of ensuring that highly capable AI systems reliably pursue goals that are beneficial to humans. In 2025, he co-authored *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies: Why Superhuman AI Would Kill Us All* with <EntityLink id="E114">Eliezer Yudkowsky</EntityLink>, a New York Times bestseller arguing that current development trajectories toward superintelligence are likely to cause human extinction, and calling for international regulatory intervention to halt such development.[^3]

Soares is frequently described as an AI "doomer"—a term he does not appear to dispute—and his views place him at the more pessimistic end of the AI safety spectrum. He maintains that AI alignment research as currently practiced is unlikely to succeed in time, that the difficulty of the problem is systematically underestimated by the broader field, and that humanity has at most one attempt to get the deployment of superintelligence right.[^4]

## Background and Education

Soares was raised Catholic in Vermont.[^5] He holds a B.S. in computer science and economics from George Washington University, where his capstone project, *Green Primer*, was a tool for suggesting cost-effective building efficiency upgrades based on inputs such as designs, materials, location, and budget.[^6]

Before joining MIRI, Soares worked as a software engineer at both Google and Microsoft, served as a research associate at the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and worked as a contractor for the US Department of Defense.[^7] His technical specializations include probability theory, decision theory, and mathematical logic, with programming experience across Haskell, Agda, Lean, Python, and other languages.[^8]

## Career at MIRI

Soares joined <EntityLink id="E202">MIRI</EntityLink> in April 2014 as a research fellow, having left Google to work full-time on AI safety—a decision he has described as motivated by his assessment that advanced AI poses a severe existential risk.[^9] He quickly gained a reputation within the organization for strategic insight and productivity, and shortly after joining became executive director, a role in which he led MIRI's research program and set its overall direction.[^10]

During his early years at MIRI, Soares was the primary author of the organization's technical research agenda on agent foundations. His 2014 report, "Agent Foundations for Aligning Superintelligence with Human Interests" (co-authored with Benya Fallenstein), helped formalize the challenge of building AI systems that remain aligned with human values even as their capabilities scale. A related paper, "Corrigibility," was presented at an AAAI workshop in 2015 and addressed the problem of ensuring that AI systems remain amenable to correction and shutdown by their operators.[^11] MIRI's research agenda was cited in the January 2015 open letter "Research Priorities for Robust and Beneficial Artificial Intelligence," which addressed risks from superintelligence and intelligence explosions.[^12]

Soares gave a talk at Google in 2017 on open problems in AI alignment, emphasizing the difficulty of the field.[^13]

In 2023, MIRI shifted its institutional focus from producing technical alignment research to issuing public and policy warnings about AI dangers. Concurrent with this transition, Soares moved from the executive director role to president, with Malo Bourgon becoming CEO.[^14]

## Views on AI Risk

### Alignment as an Unsolved Problem

Soares argues that AI systems are, in a meaningful sense, "grown rather than engineered"—their behaviors emerge from training processes in ways that are not fully understood or controlled by their developers. This opacity, he contends, makes it extremely difficult to verify whether a system's goals are genuinely aligned with human interests, as opposed to merely appearing so within the training distribution.[^15]

A core concern in his published work is the asymmetry between capability generalization and value generalization in advanced AI. According to Soares, AI capabilities—such as problem-solving—tend to generalize far beyond the contexts in which they were trained, while alignment properties do not generalize in the same robust way. This means a system trained to be helpful in narrow contexts may develop behaviors (such as resisting shutdown or deceiving operators) that are instrumentally useful for achieving its trained objectives but deeply harmful to humans.[^16]

He has expressed deep skepticism about mainstream approaches to solving alignment. In commentary on proposed techniques for instilling human-compatible values in AI systems, Soares has argued that training-target behaviors tend to be shallow, and that the underlying dispositions that emerge from training bear only a complex and indirect relationship to the intended training signal.[^17] He has stated publicly that direct alignment research looks unpromising to him across the board, though he sees value in research aimed at surfacing warning signs of misalignment that would be legible to outside observers.[^18]

### Superintelligence as a One-Shot Problem

A recurring theme in Soares' public communication is that superintelligence represents a threshold with no opportunity for iteration. Unlike most technological domains where engineers can learn from failure and improve iteratively, a misaligned superintelligent system—if deployed in the real world rather than a controlled lab environment—could, in his view, rapidly acquire control over critical resources before any corrective action could be taken. He has described this as "one try in real life," contrasting safe laboratory failures with catastrophic real-world ones where a sufficiently capable misaligned system could escape containment and act at scale.[^19]

He has cited surveys of AI researchers showing that a substantial fraction—reportedly 49% in some polls—estimate greater than a 10% probability that AI causes human extinction. He views even the more optimistic estimates circulating among AI lab leaders (such as a 2% or 25% extinction probability) as unacceptably high, comparing the situation to proceeding with a bridge construction project known to have a 25% chance of collapse.[^20]

### Policy Positions

Soares advocates for an international ban or freeze on research aimed at developing artificial superintelligence. He has proposed mechanisms such as monitoring of large GPU clusters and international treaty frameworks analogous to those governing uranium enrichment. He views the current competitive dynamics among AI labs—where companies continue development partly on the grounds that if they don't, a competitor will—as a form of collective irrationality that makes the problem harder to address through voluntary restraint alone.[^21]

His policy proposals have included suggestions that the international community be willing to take coercive action against actors that continue development in defiance of agreed limits, including in extreme scenarios. He acknowledges the geopolitical challenges this creates, particularly involving nuclear-armed states.[^22]

## *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* (2025)

In 2025, Soares and <EntityLink id="E114">Eliezer Yudkowsky</EntityLink> co-authored *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies: Why Superhuman AI Would Kill Us All*, published by Penguin. The book became a New York Times bestseller and was praised by figures including Stephen Fry and Max Tegmark, with Tegmark characterizing it as the most important book of the decade.[^23]

The book argues that continuing to develop superintelligent AI using current techniques is likely to result in human extinction within the lifetimes of people alive today. Its central claims rest on three interconnected arguments: that AI alignment is currently an unsolved problem with no clear path to solution; that a misaligned superintelligent system would have strong instrumental incentives to resist human attempts to control it; and that the rapid pace of AI development leaves little time for corrective action before a dangerous threshold is crossed.[^24]

The book also advocates separating useful narrow AI applications from the broader push toward general, open-ended intelligence, and calls for international regulatory frameworks to halt the race toward superintelligence. It includes supplementary materials addressing common objections to its central thesis.[^25]

Following its release, the book received extensive media coverage across outlets including Vanity Fair, the Financial Times, and CBS News. Soares promoted it through appearances on multiple podcasts, including *The Great Simplification* (March 2026), the Jim Rutt Show, the Botez Sisters Podcast (December 2025), and the Carnegie Endowment's *The World Unpacked* (September 2025), as well as a Harvard Science Book Talk.[^26]

## Personal Writing and Community Engagement

Soares maintains a personal blog at mindingourway.com, which is not affiliated with MIRI. Some of his essays from the blog have been compiled into book, ebook, and audiobook formats. He also maintains a small personal website at so8res.com.[^27]

He has been an active participant in the <EntityLink id="E538">LessWrong</EntityLink> and EA Forum communities, hosting an AMA on the EA Forum in 2014 and engaging in extended debates about AI timelines, moral philosophy, and the relative priority of AI safety versus other existential risks. In that 2014 AMA, he discussed MIRI's recruitment pipeline (drawing significantly from the LessWrong and *Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality* communities) and addressed critiques of MIRI's research directions.[^28] He has also published posts on LessWrong addressing technical questions about AI training dynamics and the difficulty of separating values from capabilities in training advanced systems.[^29]

In a public Q&A on the EA Forum, Soares indicated roughly 90% confidence that transformative AI would arrive within a range of 5 to 80 years absent major catastrophes, though he emphasized the difficulty of predicting exact timelines over the certainty of eventual arrival given current investment levels.[^30]

## Criticisms

### Views Characterized as Overconfident or Unfalsifiable

Critics have challenged several of Soares' foundational arguments. A LessWrong critique took issue with what it characterized as his "four background claims" about AI—in particular, the inference from human general problem-solving ability to rapid AI goal achievement across domains—arguing that these claims involve overgeneralization of the concept of "intelligence" and are not sufficiently formalized to generate reliable predictions. The critique suggested that the framework produces undue confidence in extinction scenarios without proportionate evidential grounding.[^31]

AI researcher Ben Goertzel has argued that the "everybody dies" narrative represents a failure of imagination about the nature of intelligence and the range of possible futures for advanced AI, though this characterizes his reasoning rather than documenting a failed prediction.[^32]

### Policy Proposals Criticized as Impractical

Soares' policy proposals—including global halts to advanced AI development, monitoring of GPU clusters, and potential coercive action against non-compliant actors—have been widely criticized as politically and logistically unrealistic. Critics point to competitive pressures from major AI developers, the geopolitical complexities of securing cooperation from nations including China, and the economic consequences of broad AI research freezes. A Lawfare analysis of *If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies* characterized the book's thesis as shakier than it first appears, arguing that its core claims rest on three unsettled empirical questions and that the book does not adequately account for the possibility that societal disruptions from near-term AI (such as job displacement and deepfakes) might trigger corrective responses before a catastrophic threshold is reached.[^33]

### "Doomer" Label and Institutional Critique

Some members of the AI safety research community have expressed concern that Soares' and MIRI's stance—which critics summarize as viewing the rest of the field as delusional for not accepting doom—risks dismissing potentially valuable safety work conducted at other institutions. Community participants on LessWrong have noted that scenarios in which Soares and Yudkowsky are correct about the intractability of alignment tend to imply that humans cannot do much to improve outcomes, which some find demotivating as a basis for research programs.[^34]

The book has also been criticized in some reviews as relying on a before/after dichotomy that understates the gradual nature of technological development, and as adopting an apocalyptic rhetorical register that may appeal more to those already persuaded than to skeptical policymakers or researchers.[^35]

## Key Uncertainties

- The empirical question of whether AI alignment is tractable within relevant timescales remains genuinely contested among researchers. Soares holds a more pessimistic view than many in the field, but reasonable experts disagree significantly.
- The feasibility of international coordination to halt or freeze advanced AI development is highly uncertain; no such framework currently exists or appears close to adoption.
- Soares' predictions about AI timelines are expressed as wide ranges, and no systematic record of his historical forecasts against outcomes has been compiled in available sources.
- The degree to which current large language models exhibit behaviors that constitute genuine misalignment—versus behaviors that are problematic but qualitatively different from the misalignment scenarios Soares describes—is an active area of empirical and theoretical debate.
