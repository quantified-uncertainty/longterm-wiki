## Overview

Ian Hogarth is a British entrepreneur, technology investor, and AI policy figure best known for co-founding the concert discovery platform Songkick, investing in over 50 machine learning startups, and serving as chair of the UK government's AI Safety Institute from its launch in 2023 through late 2025. He has been a prominent public voice on the risks of advanced AI, including an influential 2023 Financial Times essay warning against a race toward what he described as "God-like AI." He co-authors the annual *State of AI Report* with investor Nathan Benaich, which has become one of the most widely read free publications tracking AI progress.

Hogarth grew up in south London and attended Dulwich College before studying information engineering at the University of Cambridge, where he graduated with first-class honours. He subsequently completed an MA specialising in machine learning. His interest in AI dates to at least 2004, when he built an early computer vision system to classify cancer biopsy images. Since 2014, he has invested in more than 50 AI and machine learning startups, including <EntityLink id="E22">Anthropic</EntityLink>, and co-founded Plural Platform in 2021 as an early-stage venture capital firm focused on frontier technology. He has also been appointed a visiting professor at University College London's Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose.

## Background and Education

Hogarth was raised in south London and attended Dulwich College, where he studied maths and further maths at A-level.[^1] He went on to study information engineering at the University of Cambridge, graduating with first-class honours, and later completed an MA specialising in machine learning.[^1] After Cambridge, he spent time at Tsinghua University in Beijing learning Mandarin Chinese,[^1] and also worked in management consultancy in Singapore.[^1] His personal interests include surfing and rock climbing.[^1]

## Career

### Early Work and Songkick

In 2004, Hogarth built an early computer vision system to classify cancer biopsy images — an unusually early application of AI methods for the time.[^1] In 2007, he co-founded Songkick, a live music discovery and ticketing platform, alongside Pete Smith and Michelle You, all of whom had met at Cambridge. The venture was part of the 2007 Y Combinator programme in Boston.[^1] Songkick grew to serve approximately 15 million music fans.[^1]

Hogarth was named to Inc. magazine's 30-under-30 list in 2010, received the British Council's UK Young Music Entrepreneur of the Year award the same year, and was included in Forbes' Music 30-under-30 in 2012.[^1] In 2013, Songkick launched Detour, a crowdfunding platform for concerts.[^1] In June 2015, the company merged with CrowdSurge in a deal involving \$16.6 million in Series C funding, with Hogarth becoming co-CEO alongside Matt Jones.[^1] That same year, Songkick sued Ticketmaster for antitrust violations and anticompetitive behaviour; the case was eventually settled out of court, with \$130 million paid to shareholders.[^1] Hogarth described the litigation process as "very hard."[^1]

Also in 2010, Hogarth co-founded Silicon Milkroundabout with Pete Smith — a career fair for technology startups in East London aimed at addressing a graduate talent shortage in the sector.[^1]

### AI Investing and the State of AI Report

From 2014 onward, Hogarth began investing in AI and machine learning startups, eventually building a portfolio of more than 50 companies. His investments have included <EntityLink id="E22">Anthropic</EntityLink>, Cleo, Chorus AI, Hopin, Helsing, and Kheiron Medical, with the latter focused on AI-assisted cancer detection.[^1] Early investments were reportedly in the £5,000–£10,000 range.[^1]

In 2018, Hogarth began co-authoring the annual *State of AI Report* with Nathan Benaich of Air Street Capital. The report covers AI research progress, business developments, and policy trends, and has been described as the most widely read free publication on AI progress.[^1] The 2022 edition noted that 69% of surveyed machine learning experts called for more investment in AI safety.[^1]

In 2021, Hogarth co-founded Plural Platform alongside Wise co-founder Taavet Hinrikus. The firm operates as an early-stage venture capital vehicle targeting founders working at the frontiers of technology and science — sometimes referred to internally as "experienced founders" or "the unemployables."[^1] He also chairs Phasecraft, a quantum computing startup.[^1]

### Writing and Public Commentary

Hogarth's 2018 blog post "AI Nationalism" argued that the UK government's decision to allow the sale of <EntityLink id="E98">Google DeepMind</EntityLink> to Google had been strategically costly, and predicted that continued rapid progress in machine learning would lead to a form of AI nationalism in which countries compete over AI talent, compute, and data as strategic resources.[^1] The piece has been described as prescient given subsequent developments in US-China AI rivalry.[^1]

In April 2023, Hogarth published an essay in the Financial Times warning against a corporate race toward superintelligent AI, arguing that insufficient progress in AI alignment meant such systems could pose extreme risks — up to and including what he characterised as the obsolescence or destruction of humanity.[^1] The piece was reported as the most-read FT story on the day of publication.[^1] In November 2024, he published a further FT essay asking how Europe might build its first trillion-dollar startup, lamenting the continent's lag in AI development and revisiting the strategic loss of DeepMind to Google.[^1]

### UK Government AI Roles

On 18 June 2023, Hogarth was appointed chair of the UK government's Foundation Model Taskforce, which was launched with £100 million in initial government funding.[^1] The taskforce was modelled on the UK's COVID-19 Vaccine Taskforce in terms of operational agility and delegated authority. It was subsequently renamed the AI Safety Institute in November 2023 and then the AI Security Institute in February 2025. Hogarth held the chair position through December 2025.[^1]

The AI Safety Institute's work included evaluating frontier AI models for safety risks, developing safety standards, and building international partnerships — including a collaboration with the US AI Safety Institute.[^1] Hogarth's role placed him in a central position in the lead-up to the UK's 2023 AI Safety Summit. His advisory board included national security and machine learning experts, as well as figures such as <EntityLink id="E220">Paul Christiano</EntityLink> and Yoshua Bengio.[^1]

Upon appointment, Hogarth divested shareholdings in Conjecture, Lakera, and <EntityLink id="E22">Anthropic</EntityLink>, and also divested his investment in Faculty despite it not being directly within scope. He arranged to receive no personal financial benefit from his Stability AI shareholding. Divestment prices were fixed at the date of his June 2023 appointment to prevent any economic benefit from subsequent events. He also committed to recuse himself from procurement and investment decisions involving companies within the Institute's scope, and not to share non-public information gained through the role.[^1]

## Views on AI Safety

Hogarth has consistently argued that the pace of AI development outstrips the ability of researchers to ensure these systems are safe or aligned with human values. In his 2023 Financial Times essay, he argued that existing alignment efforts by frontier AI labs were insufficient and that near-term risks — such as AI-enabled cyberattacks — demanded immediate attention alongside longer-term concerns about superintelligent systems.[^1] He has expressed support for perspectives, such as that articulated by Timnit Gebru, that caution against building AGI without adequate safety guarantees.[^1]

More broadly, Hogarth has framed advanced AI as a general-purpose technology with transformative potential across sectors including defence, biology, and healthcare, while arguing that this breadth of impact makes the absence of robust safety frameworks particularly dangerous.[^1] The *State of AI Report* has consistently tracked investment in AI safety organisations and surveyed expert opinion on risk, helping to establish a baseline for monitoring how seriously the research community takes these concerns.[^1]

## Recognition

Hogarth was appointed a Commander of the Order of the British Empire (CBE).[^1] In 2023, TIME named him among the 100 Most Influential in AI.[^1]

## Criticisms and Concerns

### Conflicts of Interest

Upon his appointment as chair of the Foundation Model Taskforce, questions were raised about the extent of Hogarth's commercial investments in AI companies and their relationship to his regulatory role. While he divested from several firms and committed to recusal on related decisions, Byline Times raised broader concerns about the composition of the taskforce, including links to the Effective Altruism community, and whether the arrangement of public funds might benefit private interests without equivalent mitigation measures for all taskforce members.[^1]

### Taskforce Composition and AI Safety Summit

Critics questioned whether Hogarth's background as an entrepreneur and investor, rather than as a researcher, policymaker, or technologist, made him well-suited to lead an AI safety body.[^1] The taskforce was also criticised for its industry-heavy composition and for excluding public-interest groups, academics, and civil society organisations focused on AI harms to marginalised communities.[^1]

The 2023 AI Safety Summit, which Hogarth's taskforce helped to prepare, was characterised by some observers as prioritising large technology companies and Western governments over voices from the Global South or those focused on near-term AI harms.[^1]

### Plural Platform Restructuring

In 2023–2024, Plural restructured by establishing AS Plural Estonia as a new parent entity wholly owning Plural UK Management, in which Hogarth held a 20% stake. The move was described as motivated by operational efficiencies, though it drew attention amid wider concerns about technology investors relocating operations outside the UK.[^1]

### Response to EU Analysis

In response to Hogarth's 2024 Financial Times essay on European AI development, French entrepreneurs Olivier Coste and Yann Coatanlem argued that his analysis missed the point, attributing Europe's venture capital caution not to a shortage of experienced founders or capital, but to structural "failure costs" — including penalties associated with corporate restructuring and talent retention.[^1]

## Key Uncertainties

- The long-term impact of the AI Safety Institute's work under Hogarth's tenure remains to be assessed, particularly given the institutional renaming and structural changes during the period.
- The degree to which Hogarth's public warnings about advanced AI have influenced actual policy outcomes, versus contributing to broader cultural and political awareness, is not easy to disentangle.
- Plural Platform's investment activities during the period of Hogarth's regulatory appointment operated under recusal commitments, but the practical boundary between his regulatory and commercial roles was not always publicly transparent.
