## Overview

<EntityLink id="E115">Elizabeth Kelly</EntityLink> is a lawyer and policy expert best known for serving as the founding Director of the U.S. AI Safety Institute (AISI), housed within the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). She was appointed to that role in late 2023, following the announcement of AISI at the UK's AI Safety Summit in November of that year.[^1] Prior to leading AISI, she served as a Special Assistant to the President for Economic Policy at the White House National Economic Council, where she was instrumental in drafting President Biden's executive order on artificial intelligence, issued in October 2023.[^2]

Kelly's background combines legal training with technology and financial policy experience. She holds a JD from Yale Law School, an MSc in Comparative Social Policy from the University of Oxford, and a BA from Duke University. Before her White House role, she served as Senior Vice President of Growth for Capital One Investing and as a senior policy advisor on the Biden-Harris Transition Team. She is also a term member of the Council on Foreign Relations.[^3]

In 2024, she was named to the TIME100 Most Influential People in AI list. By mid-2025, she was described in public sources as the *former* director of AISI, indicating she had transitioned out of the role, reportedly amid political pressure and changes to the institute under a new administration.[^4]

## Background and Career

Kelly's path to AI policy drew on a combination of legal, economic, and technology experience. Her earlier career included work in the Obama administration on economic policy, and she later co-founded a fintech firm, United Income, which was subsequently acquired by Capital One.[^5] This background in both government and the private technology sector positioned her as a credible interlocutor between industry and federal regulators when AI governance became a national policy priority.

At the White House National Economic Council, her portfolio included financial regulation and technology policy. Her work on the Biden AI executive order was particularly significant: the order addressed labor standards, workplace equity, health and safety, data collection, and job disruption, and tied federal funding conditions to employer adoption of AI best practices.[^2]

## U.S. AI Safety Institute

### Founding and Mission

AISI was announced at the UK's global AI Safety Summit in November 2023 and formally established within NIST. Kelly was appointed director, building what she described in interviews as a "startup within government"—an interdisciplinary team including computer scientists, ethicists, and anthropologists focused on evaluating the safety and reliability of highly capable AI systems.[^1]

The institute's stated mission centers on advancing the science, practice, and adoption of AI safety. Operationally, this involves conducting fundamental research, rigorous pre-deployment testing of frontier AI models, and issuing public guidance. AISI assembled a consortium of over 200 stakeholders, including Stanford, Apple, Meta, Google, <EntityLink id="E218">OpenAI</EntityLink>, and Microsoft, to participate in its evaluation and standards work.[^5]

### Scope of Work

AISI's work spans what Kelly characterized in public remarks as both "here and now" harms and longer-horizon risks. Immediate concerns include privacy violations, synthetic content harms, child sexual abuse material (CSAM), and non-consensual intimate imagery. National security and public safety threats from advanced AI capabilities also fell within the institute's mandate, as specified by the Biden executive order.[^6]

The institute developed evaluation frameworks including "red team" testing standards, secure software frameworks, and tools for detecting synthetic content. Internationally, AISI signed a memorandum of understanding with the UK AI Safety Institute for joint testing and shared evaluation platforms, including the UK's Inspect platform, reflecting Kelly's emphasis on avoiding duplicated efforts across allied governments.[^7]

Kelly articulated the institute's philosophy in terms of a productive relationship between safety and innovation: according to a 2024 interview, she stated that "safety enables trust, which enables adoption, which enables innovation."[^6]

### Resources and Institutional Context

In 2024, Kelly acknowledged publicly that bipartisan senators had suggested AISI's funding could be substantially higher than initial allocations—with figures cited of approximately \$10 million versus a desired level of approximately \$100 million. While she stated that AISI had the resources needed to fulfill its executive order mandate, she noted that there was more Congress could do, and emphasized the importance of building enduring institutional capacity to keep pace with AI development.[^8]

AISI operated within NIST, an institution Kelly noted had a long track record—including Nobel laureates—and whose AI Risk Management Framework had already been adopted internationally. Separately, prior reporting noted that NIST facilities had faced infrastructure challenges including mold, leaks, and pests, raising questions about operational readiness for AISI's ambitious mandate.[^8]

## Departure and Policy Context

By June 2025, public sources described Kelly as the *former* director of AISI. An EA Forum post reported that she stepped down amid reports that the institute would be significantly reduced under the incoming Trump administration.[^4] The broader context was a shift in federal AI policy away from the Biden-era regulatory framework toward a posture more skeptical of safety-focused oversight structures.

## Criticisms and Concerns

No major public criticisms of Kelly personally have been documented in available sources. The AISI itself, and the broader approach it represented, operated in a contested policy environment. Critics of the Biden AI executive order from one direction argued it was too interventionist; critics from the AI safety community sometimes expressed concern that AISI's focus on near-term operational harms did not adequately address longer-term risks related to AI alignment or catastrophic outcomes.

The research data does not document Kelly or AISI taking explicit positions on AI alignment in the technical sense—the institute's public framing centered on operational harms and safety testing rather than on questions of AI goal alignment or existential risk, which are more central concerns in communities like <EntityLink id="E538">LessWrong</EntityLink> and the broader effective altruism-adjacent AI safety space.[^6]
